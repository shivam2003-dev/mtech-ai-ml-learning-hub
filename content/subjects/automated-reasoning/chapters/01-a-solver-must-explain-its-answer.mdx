title *Automated Reasoning*. The BITS Pilani WILP brochure verifies the title
and elective-pool placement, but does not publish a unit list. Propositional
logic, resolution, SAT, DPLL, certificates, and SMT are therefore proposed
teaching structure. **Prerequisite** and **Industry application** labels make
the boundary visible; they are not claims about the university syllabus.

## 01. Begin with the question a solver can defend

Suppose an engineering team asks, “Can these access rules ever let an
unapproved service reach the payment database?” A human can discuss the rules,
but a reliable answer needs more than a plausible sentence. We need a language
for assumptions, a meaning for each sentence, a search procedure, and evidence
that another person can check. Automated reasoning is the discipline of making
that chain explicit.

The useful first distinction is between a **claim** and a **certificate**. A
solver may return `sat`, meaning that it found an assignment satisfying the
encoded constraints, or `unsat`, meaning that it established that no such
assignment exists under the chosen semantics. The words are only as trustworthy
as the encoding and the algorithm. If “service is approved” was accidentally
represented as a free variable instead of a policy consequence, a fast result
can still answer the wrong question.

Let $\Gamma$ be a set of assumptions and let $\varphi$ be a proposed
conclusion. Semantic entailment is written

$$
\Gamma \models \varphi \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{every model that satisfies every formula in }\Gamma\text{ also satisfies }\varphi.
$$

This is different from asking whether $\Gamma\cup\{\varphi\}$ is satisfiable.
To test entailment with a refutation procedure, negate the conclusion and ask
whether $\Gamma\cup\{\neg\varphi\}$ is unsatisfiable. If no countermodel can
exist, the conclusion follows. If the set is satisfiable, the returned model is
a counterexample and should be shown to the person making the claim.

This chapter uses a small propositional world because it makes every step
visible. That is a **Prerequisite/Supplementary** foundation for larger systems,
not a promise that real access control, hardware verification, or planning can
be reduced safely without domain analysis. The central habit scales, however:
state the vocabulary, state the semantics, choose a sound procedure, and retain
an explanation of the answer.

## 02. Map syntax, semantics, and search

A proposition is a sentence that is either true or false in a model. Write
$p$ for “the request is authenticated,” $q$ for “the device is trusted,” and
$r$ for “the database is reachable.” A **literal** is a proposition or its
negation, such as $p$ or $\neg p$. A **clause** is a disjunction of literals,
for example $(\neg p\lor q)$. A formula in conjunctive normal form (CNF) is a
conjunction of clauses:

$$
F=(\neg p\lor q)\land(p\lor r)\land(\neg q\lor r).
$$

An assignment $M$ maps each variable to `true` or `false`. It satisfies a
clause when at least one literal in that clause is true, and it satisfies a CNF
formula when it satisfies every clause. The **SAT** problem asks whether a
formula has at least one model. A model is constructive evidence: it is a
small table that can be checked by evaluating each clause.

The word **valid** means true under every assignment. A tautology such as
$p\lor\neg p$ is valid. A contradiction such as $p\land\neg p$ has no model.
Satisfiability, validity, and entailment are related but should not be mixed:

| Question | Positive evidence | Negative evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is $F$ satisfiable? | a model of $F$ | a sound refutation of $F$ |
| Is $F$ valid? | a proof of validity | a countermodel of $F$ |
| Does $\Gamma$ entail $\varphi$? | a proof of $\Gamma\models\varphi$ | a model of $\Gamma\land\neg\varphi$ |

The search layer manipulates syntax while preserving semantics. Resolution
combines two clauses that contain complementary literals. DPLL branches on a
variable, simplifies clauses, propagates forced choices, and backtracks after a
conflict. Modern CDCL solvers add learned clauses and restart policies. SMT
solvers add theory reasoning for integers, arrays, or bit-vectors. These are
different engineering choices around the same contract: no accepted answer
should violate the formal semantics.

The map also explains why a natural-language prompt is not automatically a
logic problem. “Only trusted services may write” needs a decision about whether
“trusted” is a fact, a policy predicate, or a conclusion derived from evidence.
If time, identity, exceptions, or incomplete observations matter, propositional
logic may be too weak. A responsible modeller writes those limitations beside
the formula rather than quietly hiding them in a solver call.

## 03. Build intuition before writing a proof

Think of a CNF formula as a room of doors. Each clause says that at least one
of its literals must be true. If a clause becomes a single literal, that literal
is forced: this is a **unit clause**. If one literal in a clause is already true,
the clause is satisfied and can be removed. If every literal in a clause is
false, we have a conflict. DPLL repeatedly applies these three observations and
branches only when no immediate consequence remains.

For

$$
F=(p\lor q)\land(\neg p\lor r)\land(\neg r),
$$

the last clause forces $r=\mathrm{false}$. The second clause then forces
$p=\mathrm{false}$, and the first clause forces $q=\mathrm{true}$. The model
$(p=false,q=true,r=false)$ can be checked without replaying the search. Notice
that propagation is not a heuristic guess: it is an equivalence-preserving
simplification under the current partial assignment.

Now change the first clause to $(p\lor\neg q)$. The same propagation gives
$p=false$, then the first clause forces $q=false$. Still satisfiable. If instead
we add $(q)$, propagation forces $q=true$ and the first clause conflicts. The
conflict tells us that the assumptions cannot all hold together; it does not
identify which assumption is morally wrong. Unsatisfiable cores and proof
traces can help a reviewer inspect that question.

Resolution offers a second intuition. If one rule says “$p$ or $q$” and another
says “not $p$ or $r$,” then regardless of $p$, at least one of $q$ or $r$ must
hold. The resolvent is $(q\lor r)$. We eliminate a temporary choice while
preserving every model of the parent clauses. Repeating this operation can
derive the empty clause, written $\square$, which has no satisfying assignment.
Deriving $\square$ is a refutation of the original set.

The geometric picture for SAT is a hypercube: with $n$ variables there are
$2^n$ corners, one per total assignment. A clause removes a face containing
the assignments that make all its literals false. A SAT solver avoids visiting
most corners by using logical consequences. This picture is useful for
intuition, but it does not make worst-case search easy. The number of corners
still grows exponentially, and an apparently simple encoding can create a hard
branching structure.

## 04. State the formal rules and derive a refutation

For literals $\ell$ and $\neg\ell$, the propositional resolution rule is

$$
\frac{(\ell\lor A)\qquad(\neg\ell\lor B)}{A\lor B}.
$$

Here $A$ and $B$ are disjunctions of the remaining literals. The rule is
**sound**: if an assignment satisfies both parent clauses, then either it
makes $\ell$ true, in which case $B$ must be true, or it makes $\ell$ false, in
which case $A$ must be true. Thus it satisfies $A\lor B$. The rule is also
complete for propositional refutation when applied with suitable search: every
unsatisfiable CNF has a resolution proof of the empty clause.

Consider

$$
C_1=(p\lor q),\quad C_2=(\neg p\lor q),\quad
C_3=(p\lor\neg q),\quad C_4=(\neg p\lor\neg q).
$$

Resolve $C_1$ and $C_2$ on $p$ to obtain $C_5=(q)$. Resolve $C_3$ and $C_4$
on $p$ to obtain $C_6=(\neg q)$. Resolve $C_5$ and $C_6$ on $q$ to obtain
$C_7=\square$. Each step is locally checkable, so a small proof checker can
verify the conclusion even if a more complicated prover produced the proof.

The derivation also exposes an important distinction between a procedure and a
certificate. A solver can search using watched literals, activity scores, and
parallel workers, while its final proof is a sequence of resolution steps.
Search speed and proof trust are related but not identical. A fast unverified
binary result is weaker evidence than a slightly slower result accompanied by a
checker-readable certificate, especially in safety-critical use.

Resolution has limits. Naively generating all resolvents can cause a large
clause explosion. First-order resolution adds variables and unification, where
terms such as $f(x)$ and $f(a)$ can match by substituting $x\mapsto a$. A
substitution must respect variable scope, and the occurs-check prevents an
invalid infinite term such as $x=f(x)$. These are **Advanced extension** topics
for later chapters. They are mentioned here so that “resolution” is not
mistaken for one universal algorithm with one complexity profile.

## 05. Implement a bounded solver from first principles

The following Python 3.12+ program uses only the standard library. A clause is
a `frozenset` of signed integers: `3` means $x_3$ and `-3` means $\neg x_3$.
The solver returns a model or `None`. It performs unit propagation and chooses
the first unassigned variable; it is intentionally small enough to inspect.
Input validation rejects a clause containing both a literal and its negation,
because such a clause is already a tautology and needs a deliberate policy.

```python
from __future__ import annotations

from typing import Iterable

Clause = frozenset[int]
Formula = tuple[Clause, ...]
Model = dict[int, bool]


def normalise(clauses: Iterable[Iterable[int]]) -> Formula:
    """Validate a CNF and remove duplicate literals and clauses."""
    result: set[Clause] = set()
    for raw in clauses:
        clause = frozenset(raw)
        if any(literal == 0 for literal in clause):
            raise ValueError("literal 0 is not supported")
        if any(-literal in clause for literal in clause):
            continue  # tautology: it imposes no restriction
        result.add(clause)
    return tuple(sorted(result, key=lambda item: (len(item), tuple(sorted(item)))))


def simplify(formula: Formula, model: Model) -> Formula | None:
    """Apply a partial model; None denotes an encountered empty clause."""
    reduced: list[Clause] = []
    for clause in formula:
        if any(model.get(abs(literal)) == (literal > 0) for literal in clause):
            continue
        residual = frozenset(
            literal for literal in clause if abs(literal) not in model
        )
        if not residual:
            return None
        reduced.append(residual)
    return tuple(reduced)


def solve(clauses: Iterable[Iterable[int]]) -> Model | None:
    """Return a satisfying assignment, or None when the CNF is unsatisfiable."""
    formula = normalise(clauses)

    def search(current: Formula, model: Model) -> Model | None:
        while True:
            reduced = simplify(current, model)
            if reduced is None:
                return None
            if not reduced:
                return dict(model)
            units = [next(iter(clause)) for clause in reduced if len(clause) == 1]
            if not units:
                current = reduced
                break
            for literal in units:
                variable, value = abs(literal), literal > 0
                if variable in model and model[variable] != value:
                    return None
                model[variable] = value
            current = reduced
        variable = min(abs(literal) for clause in current for literal in clause)
        for value in (True, False):
            candidate = dict(model)
            candidate[variable] = value
            result = search(current, candidate)
            if result is not None:
                return result
        return None

    return search(formula, {})


sat = ((1, 2), (-1, 2), (1, -2))
unsat = ((1, 2), (-1, 2), (1, -2), (-1, -2))
model = solve(sat)
assert model is not None and all(
    any(model[abs(literal)] == (literal > 0) for literal in clause)
    for clause in sat
)
assert solve(unsat) is None
print(model)
```

The main invariant is simple: `model` contains assignments already committed,
and `simplify` preserves exactly the completions that can still satisfy the
formula. A `None` return means that one clause has no remaining true literal;
an empty formula means all clauses are satisfied. The recursion is complete for
finite propositional formulas because it eventually assigns every variable in
the absence of an earlier model or conflict.

This is not a production solver. Its naive scan costs repeated work, its
variable heuristic is weak, it records no proof, and Python recursion limits
bound depth. Those limitations are pedagogically useful: they make the
correctness contract visible before optimisations obscure it.

## 06. Compare library reasoning and inspect failure modes

For a library baseline, a solver such as Z3 can express the same small question
with a higher-level API. The following is a supplementary implementation, not a
replacement for understanding the clauses. Install a pinned, supported Z3
package in a virtual environment, then compare its model with the from-scratch
result.

```python
from z3 import Bool, Solver, Or, Not, sat

p, q = Bool("p"), Bool("q")
solver = Solver()
solver.add(Or(p, q), Or(Not(p), q), Or(p, Not(q)))
status = solver.check()
assert status == sat
print(solver.model())
```

The library brings robust propagation, theories, diagnostics, and decades of
engineering. It does not choose the right abstraction for the user. If the
policy includes an unmodelled emergency override, a solver can return `sat`
while the deployed system behaves differently. If a timeout is treated as
`unsat`, an operational wrapper can turn uncertainty into a dangerous false
claim. Always keep the status vocabulary distinct: `sat`, `unsat`, `unknown`,
and `timeout` are not interchangeable.

Common modelling mistakes deserve their own tests. **Vacuous truth** appears
when an implication is true because its premise was never made possible. A
policy “if a request is privileged, then it is logged” says nothing about
whether any request is privileged. **Inconsistent assumptions** make every
conclusion entailed, which is logically valid but operationally unhelpful.
**Name collisions** silently identify two entities that should be distinct.
**Unbounded integers** in an SMT model can differ from machine arithmetic with
overflow. **Closed-world assumptions** can turn missing evidence into a false
statement. Each failure should be represented by a regression test and a
written modelling decision.

The complexity story is also easy to misread. Brute-force SAT takes
$O(2^n\,m)$ evaluations for $n$ variables and $m$ clauses in the worst case.
DPLL can be dramatically faster on structured instances, but SAT is NP-complete
and no general polynomial-time guarantee is known. A benchmark result should
therefore include instance family, encoding, random seed, hardware, timeout,
and whether preprocessing was enabled. Reporting only the fastest successful
case hides the search distribution.

## 07. Make the result auditable, safe, and useful

An automated-reasoning system has at least four layers of responsibility. The
**semantic layer** defines what symbols mean. The **algorithmic layer** must be
sound for the claimed result. The **evidence layer** retains a model, proof,
unsatisfiable core, or trace. The **operational layer** controls timeouts,
resource limits, versioning, and human review. A weakness in any layer can
invalidate an otherwise elegant theorem.

For security, never let a solver output directly become a privileged action
without a policy boundary. Treat formulas and proof files as untrusted input;
limit memory and CPU, validate parser behavior, and isolate native solver
dependencies. Proof-carrying results reduce the trust placed in the search
engine, but the checker itself still needs testing. A model can disclose
identifiers or secrets if the encoding includes them, so logs should minimize
sensitive assignments and apply access controls.

For fairness and accountability, an explanation must be understandable to the
person affected by a decision. A ten-thousand-step proof is not a useful user
message. Keep a short explanation (the decisive rules or counterexample) beside
the complete machine certificate. If a rule encodes a social policy, automated
consistency does not establish that the policy is just. Review the premises,
exceptions, and impact separately.

In industry, solver services are usually wrapped with a versioned schema,
canonical serialization, deterministic options where possible, and an audit
record containing input hash, solver version, status, duration, and certificate
hash. A change in preprocessing can change a model witness without changing
logical satisfiability, so downstream systems should consume the status and
contract rather than compare raw model text. CI should include known SAT,
known UNSAT, malformed-input, timeout, and regression instances.

Research connections include CDCL clause learning, SMT DPLL(T), model checking,
planning, proof assistants, and neuro-symbolic guidance. Modern learned
heuristics can choose variables or clauses, but learning a heuristic does not
remove the need for a sound checker. A useful experiment compares a learned
choice policy to a fixed baseline on held-out instance families and reports
both speed and proof validity. The paper’s claim, the implementation’s result,
and this chapter’s educational interpretation should be labelled separately.

It is worth making the word **proof** precise in an engineering handoff. A
proof is not merely a sequence of messages saying that the solver “considered”
some alternatives. A checker needs enough information to reconstruct each
inference, and it must reject malformed or out-of-order steps. For a resolution
trace, that can mean the identifiers of two parent clauses, the pivot literal,
and the derived clause. For a model, it means a complete assignment for every
variable relevant to the formula, plus a checker that evaluates every original
clause rather than trusting a solver's pretty-printer. This small distinction
prevents an observability log from being confused with a certificate.

Proofs also have a lifecycle. A preprocessor may remove a variable, reorder
clauses, or substitute an equivalent formula before search. If the certificate
is generated for the transformed instance, the system needs a replay map back
to the user's original rules. Otherwise a reviewer can verify a true theorem
about an object they never approved. In a research notebook, store the original
text, canonical CNF, transformation options, and certificate hash together.
In a deployed service, make that bundle immutable and include the solver and
checker versions. Reproducibility is not clerical overhead; it is part of the
reasoning claim.

A countermodel deserves the same care as a proof. If a policy conclusion is
not entailed, print the smallest useful witness: the assumptions that are true,
the conclusion that is false, and the rules that remain inactive. Do not call a
single witness “the reason” when many witnesses exist. Minimising a witness is
an optimisation problem and may introduce a tie-breaking bias. Present the
selected witness with its selection rule and retain the raw model for audit.

Finally, separate logical uncertainty from operational uncertainty. `unknown`
may mean that a theory procedure is incomplete; `timeout` means the resource
budget expired; a parser error means no formula was accepted. A dashboard that
maps all three to “policy safe” destroys the semantics of the result. The
wrapper should propagate each status, alert on unexpected frequencies, and
require a human or a conservative fallback for actions that depend on a proof.

## 08. Study lab: turn a claim into an experiment

Choose a small access-control, scheduling, or configuration question that can
be stated with ten or fewer Boolean variables. Write a glossary before writing
clauses. For each rule, record its source, polarity, and whether it is a fact,
assumption, or desired conclusion. Encode the question as $\Gamma\land\neg
\varphi$, run the bounded solver, and independently evaluate any returned model.
If the result is unsatisfiable, add a proof-trace extension or compare with a
library solver; do not call a timeout a proof.

Measure at least: number of variables, clauses, propagation steps, branch
nodes, wall-clock time, and status. Vary one modelling choice, such as an
optional exception, and explain how the model or proof changes. Include a
negative test with a deliberately inconsistent assumption. The deliverable is
not a polished screenshot; it is a repository containing code, tests, input
hashes, environment versions, a short semantic specification, and a limitations
paragraph.

For a stronger study, create a tiny benchmark matrix instead of one hand-picked
formula. Include a satisfiable family with a planted model, an unsatisfiable
family formed by adding a conflicting clause, and a family with duplicated or
tautological clauses. Record whether normalisation changes the result and how
many branch nodes the baseline explores. Then add a variable-ordering heuristic
as a separate commit. The comparison should state whether a speedup comes from
fewer nodes, cheaper propagation, or simply a lucky order. This habit mirrors
larger solver papers, where an average runtime without instance distribution
can be misleading.

The lab can also ask for a minimal proof object. Extend `solve` with an event
log for decisions, unit implications, and conflicts. A student need not produce
a complete industrial DRAT certificate in this first exercise; they should be
able to replay each event and demonstrate that a reported model satisfies the
original clauses. For an UNSAT instance, mark the log as an explanatory trace
unless a checker validates a formal proof. This wording protects against a
common overclaim: a trace that explains the search is valuable, but it is not
automatically a sound proof of unsatisfiability.

The six-project ladder for this subject is proposed, not official: (1) a
beginner CNF truth-table checker; (2) a beginner DPLL visualiser; (3) an
intermediate DIMACS parser and benchmark runner; (4) an intermediate Sudoku or
schedule encoding with counterexample explanations; (5) an advanced SMT-backed
configuration verifier with proof/status logging; and (6) a research project
reproducing a CDCL heuristic paper on held-out instances. Every project should
state its data licence, threat model, metrics, tests, deployment boundary,
ethical considerations, and marking rubric.

## 09. Oral questions, summary, and what comes next

1. Why does a model prove satisfiability but not entailment? Explain the role of
   $\Gamma\land\neg\varphi$.
2. What makes resolution sound? Give the two cases for the pivot literal.
3. Why is a timeout neither `sat` nor `unsat`?
4. Which invariant does unit propagation preserve?
5. When would a proof certificate be more valuable than a fast model witness?
6. How can an inconsistent assumption set make every conclusion appear true?
7. What changes when machine integers replace mathematical integers?
8. Which part of your encoding is a documented interpretation rather than an
   official syllabus fact?

The durable idea is modest but powerful: a solver is not an oracle. It is a
program operating on a formal language under assumptions. A trustworthy answer
therefore pairs status with semantics and evidence. SAT gives us a constructive
model; refutation gives us an absence argument; DPLL exposes search; resolution
provides a local proof rule; and engineering practice turns these ideas into
bounded, versioned, reviewable services. The next chapter can add terms,
predicates, and unification, where modelling becomes richer and proof search
must manage substitutions. Before moving on, rerun the code, break one clause,
and write down exactly which claim changed.
