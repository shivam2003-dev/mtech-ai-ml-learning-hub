## 01 Begin with the decision, not the label

### The one-minute version

Calling software “intelligent” does not specify what it should do. A usable
specification names a situation, the information available in that situation,
the actions the system may take, the consequences that can follow, the
constraints that must never be traded away, and the evidence by which success
will be judged. Only after this contract exists should an engineer choose
search, logic, probability, evolution, neural learning, or a hybrid method.

This chapter treats intelligence as a question about **competent computation
under a stated task contract**, not as a magical substance inside a machine.
That framing is deliberately modest. A program may choose good delivery routes
without understanding a city as a person does. Another system may imitate
human conversation while making unsafe decisions in a warehouse. Performance
on one benchmark is evidence about that benchmark under its test conditions; it
is not automatic evidence of general competence.

Three questions discipline the discussion:

1. What decision must be made, and for whom?
2. What information and actions are genuinely available at decision time?
3. Which observations would support or refute the claim that the system helps?

### Mira's campus-delivery agent

Mira operates small delivery robots on a university campus. A robot must carry
lab samples from a collection point to a processing room. At one junction it
can take the **atrium**, **garden**, or **service corridor**. The atrium is
short but crowded. The garden is longer and may be wet. The service corridor
uses more battery but has controlled access.

“Choose the best route” sounds simple until “best” is unpacked. Does lateness
matter more than energy? Is a 20 percent obstruction risk acceptable for a
routine parcel but forbidden for a fragile sample? Does the map describe the
current building or yesterday's building? Can the robot turn back after seeing
a blocked door? What happens when every route violates the safety threshold?

The route selector is small enough to understand completely, yet it exposes
the same design obligations as a larger AI system. A model does not decide
which harms are acceptable; people encode that policy through constraints,
objectives, operating procedures, and oversight. A high test score cannot
repair a poorly chosen task.

### Four meanings that should not be collapsed

**Human imitation** asks whether behaviour resembles human behaviour. Turing's
1950 paper is historically important because it replaced an unproductive
debate over the word “think” with an operational imitation game [ACI-R14]. It
did not prove that linguistic imitation is the only definition of
intelligence.

**Rational agency** asks whether an action is expected to perform well relative
to specified information and goals. “Rational” here does not mean morally
right, all-knowing, or always successful. It means appropriately selected
under a model and evidence set. If the model is wrong or the objective omits a
harm, a locally rational action can still be a bad real-world action.

**Learning ability** asks whether performance improves through experience.
Learning is one route to competent behaviour, not a requirement for every
intelligent procedure. A carefully designed search algorithm can solve new
instances without fitting model parameters from examples.

**Computational intelligence** commonly groups adaptive paradigms such as
neural, evolutionary, and fuzzy computation. Bezdek describes the history and
contested boundaries of computational intelligence rather than presenting a
single universally accepted border [ACI-S03]. In this book, the phrase helps
compare families of methods; it is not used to exclude symbolic or
probabilistic reasoning.

The practical lesson is not to win a definition contest. It is to say which
meaning a claim uses. “The route agent is intelligent” is weak. “Under the
declared map, congestion estimates, and risk cap, the agent selects the
lowest-scored feasible route and fails safely when none exists” is inspectable.

## 02 Map the agent problem before choosing a method

An **agent** is an entity considered as receiving observations and producing
actions. The **environment** is everything relevant outside that decision
boundary. The boundary is chosen for analysis: the same robot can be treated
as one agent, as a controller plus several subsystem agents, or as part of a
human-robot team.

A useful task card contains six fields.

| Field | Delivery example | Question to audit |
|---|---|---|
| Stakeholder and decision | laboratory operations chooses a route | who benefits, bears risk, or can override? |
| Observation | predicted minutes, obstruction risk, energy estimate | was each value available before action? |
| Action | choose one legal route or request help | are unsafe actions excluded? |
| Environment response | movement, delay, obstruction, battery use | which outcomes are uncertain or delayed? |
| Performance measure | timely delivery subject to risk policy | does the measure represent the real purpose? |
| Evidence plan | replay logs, simulation, supervised pilot | which claim can each test support? |

This resembles the familiar performance-environment-actuators-sensors, or
PEAS, checklist used in introductory AI texts. The names are less important
than closing the gaps between them. A “sensor” field that says only “camera”
does not state whether darkness, occlusion, calibration drift, or privacy
filtering changes the observation. A “performance” field that says “fast” does
not state the unit, comparison, or safety constraint.

### Separate observation from hidden state

Let $s_t$ denote the relevant state of the environment at time $t$, and let
$o_t$ denote what the agent observes. These are equal only in an idealised,
fully observable task. The actual obstruction may be part of $s_t$; a camera
classification and yesterday's access log may be components of $o_t$.

Confusing observation with state produces confident but brittle designs. A
green light may be observed, while a pedestrian hidden behind a vehicle
remains part of the state. A low congestion prediction is an observation
generated by a model, not the congestion itself. Every observation has a
source, timestamp, resolution, and possible error.

Let $\mathcal{A}(o_t)$ be the set of actions legal after observation $o_t$.
The set can be smaller than the robot's physical capabilities. A robot may be
capable of entering a restricted corridor but forbidden by policy. Encoding
that rule as a hard legality or feasibility check is different from assigning
a small numerical penalty that speed could outweigh.

### Write the claim before building the system

Mira's first claim is:

> For a logged decision containing three route estimates, the selector returns
> the feasible route with minimum declared cost, rejects invalid inputs, and
> reports when no route meets the risk policy.

This is a software claim. Unit and property tests can support it. A second
claim might say:

> In a supervised four-week pilot on eligible daytime deliveries, the full
> routing system reduces median completion time without increasing
> obstruction interventions or low-battery incidents relative to the current
> dispatcher.

That is an empirical system claim. It needs a baseline, sampling and assignment
plan, enough observations, uncertainty reporting, and a review of incidents.
Passing the first claim does not establish the second. Even a successful
pilot does not establish performance at night, in another building, or after
sensor maintenance changes.

### A lightweight knowledge graph

The task card also reveals prerequisite links:

```text
observations -> state estimation -> decision rule -> action
       |                                  |
       +-> provenance                     +-> constraints

environment dynamics -> outcomes -> evaluation -> updated model or policy
```

Search becomes relevant when actions lead through many states. Probability
becomes relevant when state or outcomes are uncertain. Logic becomes useful
when relationships and rules must be represented explicitly. Learning becomes
useful when experience can improve predictions or policies. Evolutionary and
swarm methods become candidates when conventional optimisation is difficult
and approximate population-based search is acceptable. The formulation comes
first because it determines which capability is actually needed.

## 03 Classify the environment and choose an honest abstraction

Environment labels are not decorative taxonomy. They predict which assumptions
will fail and which computational tools may help.

### Fully observable versus partially observable

In a **fully observable** abstraction, the current observation contains all
state information needed to choose optimally. A board-game position can often
be modelled this way, though clocks, hidden bugs, or an opponent's psychology
may be omitted intentionally. The campus is **partially observable** because
crowds, wet surfaces, and temporary obstructions are not measured perfectly.
The agent may need a belief about possible states or a conservative fallback.

### Deterministic versus stochastic

In a **deterministic** model, a state and action fix the next state. In a
**stochastic** model, several next states have probabilities. Code can be
deterministic while its environment is stochastic: given identical inputs the
route selector returns the same route, but actual travel time still varies.
Randomness is not the only source of uncertainty; an incomplete model and an
unobserved adversary can also make outcomes uncertain.

### Episodic versus sequential

An **episodic** decision can be evaluated largely independently of earlier
actions. Image classification is sometimes abstracted this way. A
**sequential** decision changes later options. Choosing the garden route uses
battery and changes the robot's location, so a later route cannot be evaluated
as though nothing happened. Greedy one-step scoring may then be inadequate;
planning or reinforcement learning may be needed.

### Static versus dynamic

A **static** environment does not change while the agent deliberates. A
**dynamic** campus does: people move, doors close, and deadlines approach.
Computation time is therefore part of performance. An exact optimiser that
takes ten minutes to choose a five-minute route is operationally worse than a
safe approximation returned promptly.

### Discrete versus continuous

The three named routes create a **discrete** action set. Steering angle,
velocity, time, and battery voltage are naturally **continuous**. Real systems
often combine both. A high-level planner selects a corridor; a low-level
controller produces continuous motor commands. The abstraction should preserve
the variables needed for the decision while omitting detail that only increases
cost.

### Single-agent versus multi-agent

Mira's initial model treats other people and robots as environment dynamics. A
multi-agent model becomes useful when their goals and adaptations matter. Two
delivery robots may cooperate over charging slots, compete for a narrow path,
or communicate intentions. Once other agents react strategically, the
transition model cannot safely treat them as passive noise.

No classification is permanently true of a domain. It is true of a model for a
purpose. A simulator may expose its complete internal state while the deployed
robot sees only sensors. A route can be deterministic in a frozen test map and
stochastic during live operation. Good documentation records the abstraction,
its reason, and the warning signs that require revision.

## 04 Derive a constrained decision rule and follow one case

Suppose the current observation is $o$, and $F(o)$ is the set of routes
that meet non-negotiable policy constraints. For each feasible route $a$, the
agent receives three estimates:

- $t(a \mid o)$: expected travel time in minutes;
- $r(a \mid o)$: obstruction risk, represented here as a number from $0$
  to $1$;
- $e(a \mid o)$: expected energy use in watt-hours.

We define a transparent score

$$
J(a \mid o)
= w_t\,t(a \mid o)
+ w_r\,r(a \mid o)
+ w_e\,e(a \mid o),
$$

where $w_t$, $w_r$, and $w_e$ are non-negative policy weights. Their units
matter. If $J$ is interpreted as a monetary-equivalent cost, then $w_t$
has cost per minute, $w_r$ has cost per unit risk, and $w_e$ has cost per
watt-hour. Alternatively, all attributes can be normalised to dimensionless
scales before dimensionless weights are used. Quietly adding unlike physical
units without such an interpretation creates a number with no defensible
meaning.

The decision rule is

$$
a^\star(o) \in \underset{a \in F(o)}{\arg\min}\; J(a \mid o).
$$

The star marks a selected action, not a universally optimal route. The result
is conditional on the observation, estimates, feasible set, and weights. If
two routes have the same minimum score, the expression returns a set. Software
must define a stable tie policy; our implementation chooses the
lexicographically smaller name so replays are deterministic.

### Why risk is both a constraint and a score term

Let the maximum accepted obstruction risk be $\rho_{\max}$. Then

$$
F(o)=\{a\in\mathcal{A}(o):r(a\mid o)\leq\rho_{\max}\}.
$$

The hard cap prevents time or energy benefits from purchasing an explicitly
forbidden risk. The risk weight still distinguishes feasible routes below the
cap. This separation is important. Setting a very large penalty is not
identical to a prohibition because some combination of other terms can
eventually outweigh any finite penalty.

### Hand-worked delivery

Assume the following observed estimates:

| Route | Time $t$ | Risk $r$ | Energy $e$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| atrium | 8 min | 0.28 | 18 Wh |
| garden | 11 min | 0.08 | 16 Wh |
| service | 10 min | 0.04 | 25 Wh |

Let $\rho_{\max}=0.20$, $w_t=1.0$, $w_r=30.0$, and $w_e=0.10$.
The atrium is excluded because $0.28>0.20$. For the two feasible routes,

$$
\begin{aligned}
J(\text{garden}\mid o)
  &=1.0(11)+30.0(0.08)+0.10(16)\\
  &=11+2.4+1.6=15.0,\\
J(\text{service}\mid o)
  &=1.0(10)+30.0(0.04)+0.10(25)\\
  &=10+1.2+2.5=13.7.
\end{aligned}
$$

The service corridor is selected. Notice that it consumes more energy than the
garden, yet its lower predicted time and risk produce the smaller declared
cost. This is not an objective fact that service is “best.” It is the
consequence of Mira's stated policy model.

### Where the derivation stops being adequate

Weighted scalarisation assumes that a fixed exchange rate between objectives
is meaningful. Stakeholders may reject that assumption. A two-minute saving
may be valuable near a laboratory deadline but irrelevant otherwise. Risk
estimates may be poorly calibrated. A single expected value can hide a small
chance of catastrophic delay. The one-step rule ignores how the route affects
future battery state. These limitations point toward constrained optimisation,
Pareto analysis, robust decisions, planning, and sequential decision theory in
later chapters. Formalism helps because its failure conditions are visible.

## 05 Turn the contract into tested Python

The implementation below supports Python 3.12. The first block uses only the
standard library. It validates domain assumptions, separates infeasibility from
ordinary selection, and returns enough detail for an audit log.

```python
from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass
from math import isfinite
from typing import Sequence


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class RouteOption:
    name: str
    minutes: float
    risk: float
    energy_wh: float


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class DecisionWeights:
    time: float
    risk: float
    energy: float


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class DecisionResult:
    route_name: str
    score: float
    feasible_count: int


def _require_finite_nonnegative(value: float, label: str) -> None:
    if not isfinite(value) or value < 0:
        raise ValueError(f"{label} must be finite and non-negative")


def _validate_inputs(
    options: Sequence[RouteOption],
    weights: DecisionWeights,
    max_risk: float,
) -> None:
    if not options:
        raise ValueError("at least one route option is required")
    if not isfinite(max_risk) or not 0 <= max_risk <= 1:
        raise ValueError("max_risk must be between 0 and 1")

    names: set[str] = set()
    for option in options:
        if not option.name.strip():
            raise ValueError("route names must be non-empty")
        if option.name in names:
            raise ValueError("route names must be unique")
        names.add(option.name)
        _require_finite_nonnegative(option.minutes, "minutes")
        _require_finite_nonnegative(option.energy_wh, "energy_wh")
        if not isfinite(option.risk) or not 0 <= option.risk <= 1:
            raise ValueError("risk must be between 0 and 1")

    for label, value in (
        ("time weight", weights.time),
        ("risk weight", weights.risk),
        ("energy weight", weights.energy),
    ):
        _require_finite_nonnegative(value, label)
    if weights.time + weights.risk + weights.energy == 0:
        raise ValueError("at least one weight must be positive")


def select_route_first_principles(
    options: Sequence[RouteOption],
    weights: DecisionWeights,
    max_risk: float,
) -> DecisionResult:
    """Return the minimum-score feasible route with a stable name tie-break."""
    _validate_inputs(options, weights, max_risk)
    feasible = [option for option in options if option.risk <= max_risk]
    if not feasible:
        raise RuntimeError("no route satisfies the risk policy")

    scored = [
        (
            weights.time * option.minutes
            + weights.risk * option.risk
            + weights.energy * option.energy_wh,
            option.name,
        )
        for option in feasible
    ]
    score, name = min(scored)
    return DecisionResult(name, score, len(feasible))
```

A library implementation can express the same operation over arrays. It
requires NumPy 2.x in addition to Python 3.12. Vectorisation can improve the
constant factors for a large batch, but it does not change the policy or repair
invalid estimates.

```python
from __future__ import annotations

from typing import Sequence

import numpy as np


def select_route_numpy(
    options: Sequence[RouteOption],
    weights: DecisionWeights,
    max_risk: float,
) -> DecisionResult:
    """Vectorised equivalent of the first-principles selector."""
    _validate_inputs(options, weights, max_risk)
    values = np.asarray(
        [(item.minutes, item.risk, item.energy_wh) for item in options],
        dtype=np.float64,
    )
    feasible_indices = np.flatnonzero(values[:, 1] <= max_risk)
    if feasible_indices.size == 0:
        raise RuntimeError("no route satisfies the risk policy")

    weight_vector = np.asarray(
        [weights.time, weights.risk, weights.energy],
        dtype=np.float64,
    )
    scores = values[feasible_indices] @ weight_vector
    candidates = sorted(
        (float(scores[position]), options[index].name)
        for position, index in enumerate(feasible_indices)
    )
    score, name = candidates[0]
    return DecisionResult(name, score, int(feasible_indices.size))
```

The executable tests below include the hand calculation, cross-implementation
agreement, boundary behaviour, invalid data, infeasibility, and deterministic
ties. They are ordinary assertions so the chapter can be tested without a
framework.

```python
from __future__ import annotations

from math import isclose


def _expect_exception(error_type: type[BaseException], action: object) -> None:
    try:
        action()  # type: ignore[operator]
    except error_type:
        return
    raise AssertionError(f"expected {error_type.__name__}")


def run_tests() -> None:
    options = [
        RouteOption("atrium", 8.0, 0.28, 18.0),
        RouteOption("garden", 11.0, 0.08, 16.0),
        RouteOption("service", 10.0, 0.04, 25.0),
    ]
    weights = DecisionWeights(time=1.0, risk=30.0, energy=0.10)

    basic = select_route_first_principles(options, weights, 0.20)
    assert basic.route_name == "service"
    assert isclose(basic.score, 13.7)
    assert basic.feasible_count == 2

    vectorised = select_route_numpy(options, weights, 0.20)
    assert vectorised == basic

    _expect_exception(
        ValueError,
        lambda: select_route_first_principles([], weights, 0.20),
    )
    _expect_exception(
        ValueError,
        lambda: select_route_first_principles(
            [options[0], RouteOption("atrium", 9.0, 0.10, 12.0)],
            weights,
            0.20,
        ),
    )
    _expect_exception(
        ValueError,
        lambda: select_route_first_principles(
            [RouteOption("bad", float("nan"), 0.10, 12.0)],
            weights,
            0.20,
        ),
    )
    _expect_exception(
        ValueError,
        lambda: select_route_first_principles(
            [RouteOption("bad", 9.0, 1.10, 12.0)],
            weights,
            0.20,
        ),
    )
    _expect_exception(
        RuntimeError,
        lambda: select_route_first_principles(options, weights, 0.01),
    )

    tied = [
        RouteOption("zeta", 5.0, 0.10, 5.0),
        RouteOption("alpha", 5.0, 0.10, 5.0),
    ]
    assert select_route_first_principles(tied, weights, 0.20).route_name == "alpha"


if __name__ == "__main__":
    run_tests()
    print("8 route-selection tests passed")
```

Expected output:

```text
8 route-selection tests passed
```

For $n$ routes and $k=3$ attributes, the explicit implementation takes
$O(nk)$, conventionally simplified to $O(n)$ because $k$ is fixed. It
stores $O(n)$ feasible and scored entries. A streaming implementation could
reduce auxiliary space to $O(1)$ while retaining the current best route.
The NumPy version also performs $O(nk)$ arithmetic and allocates an
$n\times k$ array. For only three routes, clear standard-library code is
likely preferable; vectorisation becomes useful when many decisions are
evaluated in batches.

Tests establish conformance to the declared rule, not the truth of the inputs.
They cannot prove that a congestion model is calibrated, a risk cap is ethical,
or a route remains open. The production system must record model versions,
observation timestamps, chosen route, rejected alternatives, constraint
results, and any human override without retaining unnecessary personal data.

## 06 Learn from what goes wrong

### Proxy failure

An objective is a proxy for a purpose. If Mira optimises only travel time, the
robot may choose crowded paths, accelerate abruptly, or arrive with too little
battery for a safe return. Adding terms does not automatically solve the
problem: the selected metrics may still omit discomfort, accessibility, or
rare severe events. Amodei and colleagues catalogue concrete safety problems
that can arise when learning systems pursue imperfect objectives or encounter
distribution shift [ACI-R26]. Their examples motivate analysis; they do not
imply that every route selector is a learning agent.

The remedy begins with a hierarchy:

1. define non-negotiable constraints;
2. define measurable objectives inside the feasible region;
3. monitor side effects not captured by the objective;
4. provide an escalation path when the model is uncertain or infeasible.

### Partial observability disguised as certainty

The score accepts one risk number per route. In reality that number is an
estimate with uncertainty. If the camera is offline, replacing the missing
value with zero silently converts ignorance into safety. A safer interface
might reject stale observations, attach confidence intervals, or route the
decision to a human. Missingness has operational meaning and must not be
treated as a formatting inconvenience.

### Distribution shift

A garden-risk model trained in dry weather may fail during monsoon conditions.
A new building layout changes travel times. Students leaving an event produce
crowd patterns absent from historical logs. The input still has the correct
shape, so validation passes, but the relationship between features and
outcomes has changed. Monitoring should therefore compare operating conditions
with the conditions represented in evaluation and trigger a conservative mode
when the difference matters.

### Benchmark overreach

A benchmark compresses a task into data, procedure, and metrics. It makes
comparison possible, but it also creates an incentive to optimise what is
measured. A route planner that excels on static maps may fail when sensor
latency and human motion are introduced. Raji and Dobbe argue for reasoning
about the boundaries and validity of AI evaluation rather than treating a
leaderboard score as universally informative [ACI-S02].

For each benchmark result, write a claim card:

- system and version tested;
- population or scenario represented;
- input information supplied;
- intervention or action permitted;
- primary and safety metrics;
- baseline and uncertainty;
- exclusions and known gaps;
- conditions under which the claim should not be reused.

### Automation bias and responsibility gaps

People may follow a system recommendation because it looks precise. Displaying
“13.700” can create unwarranted confidence when the travel estimate itself is
uncertain by several minutes. Interfaces should communicate the decision basis,
meaningful uncertainty, and the availability of override. Override logs should
be studied as evidence, not treated as disobedience.

Responsibility cannot be delegated to an equation. Product owners set the
purpose and acceptable risk; domain experts define operational constraints;
engineers implement and test; operators supervise; reviewers examine impacts;
and an accountable authority decides whether deployment remains justified.
The NIST AI Risk Management Framework is a useful industry-oriented structure
for governing, mapping, measuring, and managing such risks [ACI-R08].

## 07 Evaluate evidence, cost, and responsibility

Evaluation should proceed from the narrowest claim to the broadest, stopping
when evidence no longer supports the next step.

### Level 1: software correctness

Unit tests verify input validation, score calculation, feasibility, ties, and
failure behaviour. Property tests could add invariants: tightening the risk cap
must never make an infeasible route feasible; increasing one route's time while
holding everything else fixed must not improve its score. Static analysis and
dependency scanning address implementation quality. None of these tests
validate the environment model.

### Level 2: model and data validity

Replay historical decisions while preserving the information available at
each original decision time. Compare predicted and realised time, energy, and
obstruction outcomes. Examine calibration across route, time of day, weather,
and accessibility conditions. Separate a tuning set from the final evaluation
set. Record exclusions and missing outcomes. Averages should be accompanied by
distributions and rare-event counts.

Historical replay remains counterfactual: logs reveal the outcome of the route
that was taken, not necessarily outcomes for rejected routes. Simulation can
exercise alternatives but inherits simulator assumptions. This is why multiple
forms of evidence are stronger than one large retrospective score.

### Level 3: supervised pilot

Begin with low-consequence deliveries and a human supervisor. Compare against
the current dispatch method using a predeclared primary metric and safety
guardrails. Report uncertainty and operational incidents, not only statistical
significance. A pilot should include a stop condition for sensor failure,
unexpected interaction, or degraded battery behaviour.

### Level 4: monitored operation

Deployment changes the environment. People adapt to robot routes, maintenance
changes sensors, and usage expands. Monitor inputs, outcomes, overrides,
near-misses, latency, and resource cost. Schedule review by time and by trigger.
A stable dashboard is not proof of a stable world; logging failure can make
incidents disappear from the metric.

### Security, privacy, accessibility, and fairness

Route observations may contain images or device identifiers. Collect only what
the decision and audit require, restrict access, define retention, and avoid
publishing traces that reveal personal routines. Authenticate map and policy
updates because a manipulated risk estimate could redirect robots. Rate-limit
and validate external inputs. Fail closed for prohibited spaces and fail
safely, through stop or escalation, when all routes are infeasible.

Accessibility is part of the environment contract. A robot that repeatedly
occupies ramps or narrows a passage can impose unequal costs even if average
delivery time improves. Evaluation should stratify relevant outcomes and invite
affected users to review the operating policy. “Fairness” is not one universal
number; the metric must correspond to a stated harm, group, and decision.

### Reproducibility ledger

For each evaluation retain:

- code commit and dependency lock;
- policy, weights, thresholds, and model versions;
- dataset or simulator version and inclusion rules;
- deterministic seeds where randomness is used;
- environment configuration and hardware class;
- metrics, uncertainty method, and baseline;
- failed runs, incidents, and manual interventions;
- reviewer, date, limitations, and approved claim text.

This ledger turns “the system worked” into evidence another reviewer can
inspect. It also enables rollback when a later change degrades performance.

### Study lab: route policy under uncertainty

**Industry application.** Build a small decision notebook or command-line tool
around the tested selector.

1. Create at least twenty synthetic decisions with three to six routes each.
   Include stale observations, missing values, infeasible cases, and exact

2. Declare two stakeholder policies with different weights and risk caps.
   Explain the units or normalisation behind each weight.
3. Run both policies and report how often their selected routes differ.
4. Perturb each estimate by a plausible error range. Identify decisions whose

5. Design a fallback for no feasible route and for unavailable risk evidence.

### Solution guidance

A strong answer will not choose weights before describing stakeholder conflict.
It will distinguish a room being free in the database from being usable in the
physical world. Accessibility and safety constraints should not be purchasable
with a scheduling-efficiency gain. The rule may minimise a cost, maximise a
utility, or solve a constrained allocation, but it must include an infeasible
case. Evaluation should compare with the current allocation process and inspect
outcomes across relevant groups and room types. The final claim should concern
the tested scenario, not “intelligent scheduling” in general.

## 09 Defend the chapter orally and carry it forward

### Oral questions with concise answers

**1. Is an agent necessarily a robot?**

No. An agent is an analytical abstraction that maps information to action. A
software scheduler, game program, robot, or human-software team can be modelled
as an agent when that boundary helps answer the question.

**2. Why is rationality not the same as intelligence?**

Rationality evaluates action relative to specified information and goals.
Intelligence has several contested meanings, including imitation, adaptation,
reasoning, and competence across tasks. A rationality model is useful because
its assumptions can be stated, not because it settles every definition.

**3. Why not place every concern in one weighted score?**

Some policies are constraints, not trade-offs. A finite safety penalty can be
outweighed by enough benefit elsewhere. Hard feasibility checks express that
certain actions are unacceptable regardless of their other score terms.

**4. What makes the campus partially observable?**

Relevant state such as temporary obstructions and human motion is not measured
perfectly at decision time. Sensor output is evidence about state, not state
itself.

**5. What do the Python tests prove?**

They support the claim that the implementation follows the declared rule for
the tested cases and rejects specified invalid inputs. They do not prove that
weights, estimates, or deployment policy are correct.

**6. Why can a deterministic program have uncertain outcomes?**

The program can return the same action for the same inputs while the
environment responds stochastically or contains unobserved state.

**7. When does one-step route scoring become inadequate?**

When an action changes important future choices, outcomes have long horizons,
uncertainty must be represented explicitly, or the exchange between objectives
is not captured by fixed weights.

**8. How should a no-feasible-route case be handled?**

As an explicit operational state: stop, request help, or follow a separately
approved safe fallback. Silently selecting the least unsafe prohibited action
changes the policy.

### Chapter summary

An intelligent-system claim becomes useful when it is translated into a task
contract. That contract names stakeholders, observations, hidden state,
actions, environment responses, objectives, constraints, and evidence.
Environment classifications expose modelling assumptions. A formal decision
rule connects those assumptions to code, while validation and tests establish
only software-level claims. Broader claims require calibrated data, baselines,
pilots, uncertainty, incident review, and continuing monitoring.

The delivery example also showed the boundaries of a weighted score. Units and
tie policies must be explicit; non-negotiable risks belong in constraints; no
feasible action needs a safe path; and point estimates can hide uncertainty.
The next planned chapter uses the same task discipline to represent states,
successors, path costs, and heuristics for search.

### Key terms

- **Action:** an intervention available to the agent.
- **Agent:** a chosen decision boundary that maps observations to actions.
- **Benchmark:** a specified dataset or environment, protocol, and metric used for comparison.
- **Constraint:** a condition that an acceptable action or plan must satisfy.
- **Environment:** external state and dynamics relevant to the agent's task.
- **Feasible set:** actions satisfying the declared constraints.
- **Observation:** information available to the agent, possibly incomplete or noisy.
- **Performance measure:** declared evidence used to judge task outcomes.
- **Proxy:** a measurable quantity used to represent a broader purpose.
- **Rational action:** an action selected appropriately relative to stated information and objectives.
- **State:** a representation of environment information relevant to future evolution or decisions.
