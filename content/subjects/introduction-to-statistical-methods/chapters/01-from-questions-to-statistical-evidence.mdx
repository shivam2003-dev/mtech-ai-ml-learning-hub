## 01 Begin with the decision, not the dataset

### The one-minute version

A statistical analysis is a chain of meaning. A decision creates a question.
The question identifies a population and a quantity of interest. A measurement
process turns part of that population into recorded variables. A selection
process determines which records reach the dataset. An estimator converts the
sample into a number. Only then can an analyst discuss uncertainty and make a
carefully limited claim.

If any link is vague, more computation does not repair the chain. Ten million
rows measured with the wrong definition still answer the wrong question. A
beautiful confidence interval around a convenience sample can be precise about
the sample while being misleading about the population. A predictive model
evaluated on information copied from the future can score well without being
useful in deployment.

The central habit of this chapter is therefore simple:

> Before calculating, write down who or what the data represent, how the
> records were produced, which quantity answers the question, and what evidence
> would expose a mistake.

### Mira's alert-triage question

Mira works on a cloud-operations team. The team has introduced a new alert
summary intended to reduce the time between an alert arriving and an engineer
starting a useful investigation. A dashboard shows that the average recorded
triage time fell from 18 minutes to 12 minutes. A manager asks, “Did the new
summary improve triage by six minutes?”

The arithmetic is correct, but the conclusion is not yet licensed. The old
summary was mostly used during night shifts, while the new summary was piloted
by a trained day-shift group. Unresolved alerts have no final triage time. A
recent logging change starts the clock later than the old system did. Several
alerts from the same incident appear as separate rows. Engineers could see the
new summary before deciding whether an alert was actionable.

Each detail changes the question. Is the population all future alerts, only
alerts handled by the pilot group, or incidents rather than alert rows? Is the
outcome elapsed wall-clock time, active engineer time, or time to the first
correct action? Does “improve” mean a descriptive change, an association after
adjustment, or a causal effect of assigning the new summary? How should
unresolved alerts be represented? Which version of the logging system produced
each value?

This chapter follows Mira as she turns the dashboard claim into an estimand
card, checks the route from real events to recorded rows, derives what random
sampling would buy, and builds a small Python audit. The chapter does not
pretend that one audit proves causality. Its promise is more useful: by the end,
you will know exactly what must be true before the six-minute difference can
support a larger claim.

### What you should be able to defend

After the chapter, you should be able to answer these questions aloud:

1. What is the target population?
2. What is one observational unit?
3. What sampling frame was actually available?
4. How were units selected into the sample?
5. What does each variable mean, in which units, and at what time was it known?
6. What is the estimand?
7. Which estimator produced the reported number?
8. Which assumptions connect the estimate to the estimand?
9. Which failure modes could reverse the conclusion?
10. Is the result exploratory, confirmatory, descriptive, predictive, or causal?

Those questions are not administrative overhead. They are the interface
between an applied problem and statistical mathematics.

## 02 See the complete evidence map

Statistical vocabulary is easiest to remember as a map rather than a glossary.
The map has five layers.

```text
decision and context
  -> target population and unit
  -> measurement and selection processes
  -> estimand and estimator
  -> uncertainty, evaluation, and claim
```

### Layer 1: decision and context

A decision says what action may change after the analysis. Mira's team might
keep the new summary, revise it, run a randomized trial, or abandon it. The
costs of false optimism and false pessimism are different. Shipping an
ineffective summary wastes attention; rejecting a useful summary loses time.
The decision context therefore shapes the estimand, acceptable uncertainty,
guardrails, and data requirements.

An analysis without a decision can still be scientific or descriptive, but it
needs a purpose. “Explore the data” is an activity, not a complete question.
A stronger exploratory purpose is “identify plausible measurement failures and
candidate relationships that a later study should test.”

### Layer 2: population, unit, frame, and sample

The **target population** is the collection of units about which the desired
claim is made. A unit might be a person, incident, device, request, session,
model run, organisation, or time window. The unit must match the claim. If
Mira's outcome is time to investigate an incident, treating duplicate alert
rows as independent units gives busy incidents extra weight.

The **sampling frame** is the operational list or mechanism from which units
could actually be selected. The target population might be all future
production incidents, while the frame contains only incidents recorded by one
logging service during a four-week pilot. The gap between population and frame
is a coverage problem.

The **sample** is the set of observed units used by the analysis. A large
sample reduces some forms of random variation, but it does not automatically
repair a narrow frame or a selective inclusion mechanism.

### Layer 3: variables and measurement

A variable is not merely a column name. It is a rule for turning an attribute
or event into a recorded value. A useful data dictionary states:

- conceptual meaning;
- data type and legal values;
- unit of measurement;
- time of measurement;
- source system and version;
- missing-value meaning;
- transformations and aggregation;
- whether the value was available at the intended decision time.

For Mira, `triage_minutes` could mean wall-clock time from alert creation to
first ticket edit. If the clock starts at different events under two logger
versions, the groups do not share one measurement rule. Renaming both columns
to the same label does not make them comparable.

### Layer 4: estimand, estimator, and estimate

An **estimand** is the precise population quantity the analysis seeks. An
**estimator** is a rule that uses sample data to estimate that quantity. An
**estimate** is the numerical result obtained from one sample.

Suppose Mira chooses a descriptive estimand:

$$
\theta
=
\mu_{\text{new,pilot}}
-
\mu_{\text{old,pilot}},
$$

where each $\mu$ is the mean recorded triage time among eligible pilot alerts
under the corresponding logging definition. The difference of sample means is
an estimator, $\hat{\theta}$. The observed value, perhaps $-6$ minutes, is an
estimate. That estimate does not by itself equal the effect of assigning the
new summary to all future incidents.

The distinction prevents a common error: discussing the properties of a
population target as if they were properties of a particular algorithm, or
discussing one observed number as if it were the target itself.

### Layer 5: uncertainty, evaluation, and claim

An estimate changes across samples, measurement choices, model assumptions,
and random seeds. Later chapters develop standard errors, intervals, tests,
resampling, and model diagnostics. Chapter 1 asks the questions that those
methods presuppose:

- What source of variation is represented?
- Which observations can reasonably be treated as separate units?
- Which selection mechanism is assumed?
- Which errors are random, and which are systematic?
- Which analysis choices were fixed before seeing the outcomes?
- What population and time period does the claim cover?

The [NIST/SEMATECH Engineering Statistics Handbook](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/software/dataplot/handbook.htm)
organises statistical work around problem definition, data, assumptions,
methods, and diagnostics. The [MIT Statistics for Applications course](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-650-statistics-for-applications-fall-2016/)
provides the later inferential machinery. Here we build the contract those
methods need.

## 03 Define the objects before choosing a method

Let the finite target population be

$$
\mathcal{U}=\{1,2,\ldots,N\},
$$

where $N$ is the number of units. For unit $i$, let $Y_i$ denote the outcome
defined by a fixed measurement rule. The population mean is

$$
\mu
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_i.
$$

A sample $\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{U}$ contains $n$ units. Its sample mean
is

$$
\bar{Y}_{\mathcal{S}}
=
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}Y_i.
$$

The formulas are short, but every symbol carries a substantive choice. If
$i$ indexes alert rows rather than incidents, $\mu$ describes alert-weighted
time. If unresolved alerts are excluded, $\mathcal{U}$ silently becomes the
population of resolved alerts. If $Y_i$ is measured differently by logger
version, the summands do not represent a stable construct.

### The estimand card

Mira writes an estimand card before opening the analysis notebook:

| Field | Mira's provisional definition |
|---|---|
| Decision | Whether to run a controlled deployment of the new summary |
| Target population | Eligible production incidents handled by the operations team during the next quarter |
| Unit | One incident, after deduplicating linked alert rows |
| Exposure | Assignment to old or new summary at incident creation |
| Outcome | Minutes from a common incident-created timestamp to first verified investigation action |
| Summary measure | Difference in population mean outcome between assignment policies |
| Time horizon | Investigation action observed within 24 hours |
| Missingness rule | Incidents without an action remain censored or unresolved; they are not silently deleted |
| Guardrails | Wrong-action rate, unresolved rate, engineer workload, severe-incident subgroup |
| Claim type | Causal only after a defensible assignment and analysis design; otherwise descriptive |

The card exposes gaps. The current pilot used different shifts rather than a
shared assignment mechanism, so it can support a descriptive comparison of the
pilot groups but not the stated causal estimand without stronger assumptions.
That is a productive conclusion: the analysis has identified the next study
the team needs.

### Units, clusters, and pseudo-replication

Rows are not automatically independent observational units. Ten alerts from
one incident may share a cause, engineer, deployment, and outcome. Treating
them as ten unrelated incidents exaggerates the effective amount of
information. Later chapters handle cluster-aware standard errors and design
effects. At this stage, document the hierarchy:

```text
quarter
  -> deployment
    -> incident
      -> alert
        -> event log entry
```

Then choose the unit matching the estimand. Lower-level records may still be
used to construct variables, but the analysis must not confuse event count
with the number of independent incident-level units.

### Variable roles are contextual

The same column can play different roles:

- an **outcome** if it measures the response of interest;
- an **exposure** or treatment indicator if it records the policy applied;
- a **confounder candidate** if it precedes both exposure and outcome;
- a **mediator candidate** if the exposure may change it on the path to the outcome;
- a **stratification variable** if the design intentionally balances it;
- an **identifier** if it names a unit but should not become a numerical feature;
- a **post-outcome variable** that can leak future information.

These labels depend on time and question, not just dtype. `engineer_id` is a
string identifier, but it may also reveal shift allocation. `incident_closed`
is Boolean, but if it occurs after the triage decision, using it to predict
triage success would leak future information.

### Measurement error is not one generic problem

Write a simple measurement model:

$$
X_i = Y_i + E_i,
$$

where $Y_i$ is the intended quantity, $X_i$ is the recorded value, and $E_i$
is measurement error. If errors average to zero under a stable process, repeated
measurement may reduce some noise. If a new logger systematically starts the
clock three minutes later, then $E_i$ has a group-dependent component. A larger
sample makes the systematic difference more precisely visible; it does not
make the recorded groups comparable.

This additive model is only an educational simplification. Real measurement
can involve rounding, censoring, thresholds, category changes, missing values,
and human judgement. Its purpose is to force the question: which part of the
observed difference might come from how the variable was created?

## 04 Derive what random sampling buys

This section is **Supplementary**. It assumes expectation and indicator
notation from Mathematical Foundations for Machine Learning.

Suppose $\mathcal{U}$ is a fixed finite population of $N$ units with fixed
values $Y_1,\ldots,Y_N$. Select a simple random sample of exactly $n$ distinct
units, meaning that every size-$n$ subset is equally likely. Define the
inclusion indicator

$$
I_i
=
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if unit } i \text{ is selected},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

The sample mean can be rewritten as

$$
\bar{Y}_{\mathcal{S}}
=
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_iY_i.
$$

Under simple random sampling, each unit has inclusion probability

$$
\Pr(I_i=1)=\frac{n}{N}.
$$

Take expectation over the sampling mechanism. The population values remain
fixed:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}}\left[\bar{Y}_{\mathcal{S}}\right]
&=
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}}\left[
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_iY_i
\right] \\
&=
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}}[I_i]Y_i \\
&=
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\frac{n}{N}Y_i \\
&=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_i \\
&=
\mu.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore the sample mean is unbiased for the finite-population mean under this
design:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}}
\left[\bar{Y}_{\mathcal{S}}-\mu\right]=0.
$$

The result is conditional on the stated population, stable measurement, and
simple random sampling. It does not say every realised sample mean equals
$\mu$. It does not apply automatically to the first $n$ database rows, people
who volunteer, records surviving a filter, or incidents with complete outcomes.
It does not convert a descriptive population mean into a causal effect.

### A hand-worked population

Take six incident triage times:

$$
(4,6,8,10,12,20).
$$

The population mean is

$$
\mu=\frac{4+6+8+10+12+20}{6}=10.
$$

Suppose the table is sorted by severity and a convenience process takes the
first three rows. The sample mean is

$$
\bar{Y}_{\text{first three}}
=
\frac{4+6+8}{3}
=
6.
$$

The four-minute difference is not random evidence from equal-probability
sampling; it is a consequence of the selection rule applied to an ordered
frame.

Now list all size-three samples. There are

$$
\binom{6}{3}=20
$$

possible subsets. Under simple random sampling, their sample means vary, but
their average equals 10. Some realised samples remain far from 10. Unbiasedness
is a repeated-sampling property, not a guarantee for one sample.

### Selection indicators reveal convenience bias

For a general selection process, write

$$
\pi_i=\Pr(I_i=1).
$$

If inclusion probabilities differ and the ordinary mean is still used, then

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_iY_i
\right]
=
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_iY_i,
$$

which need not equal $\mu$. Units with large $\pi_i$ contribute more in
expectation. If easy incidents are more likely to be resolved and therefore
recorded, the complete-case mean overrepresents easy incidents.

Unequal-probability sampling can be principled when inclusion probabilities
are known and appropriate weighting is used. Chapter 3 will develop sampling
designs. The lesson here is narrower: an estimator must match the selection
mechanism. “We have many rows” is not a design.

### Sampling error versus selection bias

Sampling error is the difference between an estimate and its target caused by
which eligible units happen to be selected under the design. Selection bias
arises when the expected estimate under the actual selection process misses
the target. Increasing $n$ often reduces sampling variation. It may leave
selection bias unchanged.

That distinction explains why operational datasets can be enormous and still
misleading. Their size is evidence about computational scale, not automatically
about representation.

## 05 Turn the design into tested Python

The smallest useful implementation separates two jobs:

1. compute a mean under an explicit numerical contract;
2. compare a convenience selection with a seeded simple random sample when the
   teaching population is fully known.

The second job is a simulation audit, not a production proof. In real work the
target population outcomes are usually not all observed, so population error
cannot be calculated directly.

### First-principles mean

The following Python 3.12 function uses only the standard library. It rejects
empty, Boolean, nonnumeric, non-finite, and unrepresentable inputs rather than
silently manufacturing a result.

```python
from __future__ import annotations

import math
from collections.abc import Iterable


def mean_from_scratch(values: Iterable[float]) -> float:
    """Return the arithmetic mean of finite real values.

    Time complexity: O(n). Additional space: O(1).
    """
    total = 0.0
    count = 0

    for position, value in enumerate(values):
        if isinstance(value, bool):
            raise TypeError(f"values[{position}] must be a real number, not bool")
        try:
            numeric = float(value)
        except (TypeError, ValueError, OverflowError) as exc:
            raise TypeError(
                f"values[{position}] must be representable as a finite float"
            ) from exc
        if not math.isfinite(numeric):
            raise ValueError(f"values[{position}] must be finite")

        total += numeric
        if not math.isfinite(total):
            raise OverflowError("the running sum is not finite")
        count += 1

    if count == 0:
        raise ValueError("values must contain at least one observation")

    return total / count
```

For the six values above, the expected result is:

```text
10.0
```

Floating-point addition is not exact real arithmetic. For very long or
ill-scaled sequences, `math.fsum` can reduce summation error. The explicit loop
is retained here because it makes validation, accumulation, and complexity
visible.

### NumPy sampling audit

NumPy supplies a tested random generator and array operations. The audit keeps
the population one-dimensional, forbids replacement, sorts sampled indices
only for readable evidence, and returns the indices so the selection can be
reproduced.

```python
from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass
from collections.abc import Sequence

import numpy as np
from numpy.typing import NDArray


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class SamplingAudit:
    population_mean: float
    convenience_mean: float
    random_sample_mean: float
    convenience_error: float
    random_sample_error: float
    random_indices: tuple[int, ...]


def sampling_audit(
    outcomes: Sequence[float] | NDArray[np.floating],
    sample_size: int,
    *,
    seed: int = 2026,
) -> SamplingAudit:
    """Compare first-row convenience selection with simple random sampling.

    The full population is known only for this educational simulation.
    Time complexity: O(N); additional space: O(N).
    """
    try:
        population = np.asarray(outcomes, dtype=np.float64)
    except (TypeError, ValueError, OverflowError) as exc:
        raise TypeError(
            "outcomes must be numeric and representable as float64"
        ) from exc

    if population.ndim != 1:
        raise ValueError(
            f"outcomes must have exactly one axis; received {population.shape}"
        )
    if population.size == 0:
        raise ValueError("outcomes must contain at least one observation")
    if not np.isfinite(population).all():
        raise ValueError("outcomes must contain only finite values")
    if isinstance(sample_size, bool) or not isinstance(sample_size, int):
        raise TypeError("sample_size must be an integer")
    if not 1 <= sample_size <= population.size:
        raise ValueError("sample_size must be between 1 and the population size")
    if isinstance(seed, bool) or not isinstance(seed, int):
        raise TypeError("seed must be an integer")

    population_mean = float(np.mean(population))
    convenience = population[:sample_size]

    generator = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    random_indices_array = np.sort(
        generator.choice(population.size, size=sample_size, replace=False)
    )
    random_sample = population[random_indices_array]

    convenience_mean = float(np.mean(convenience))
    random_sample_mean = float(np.mean(random_sample))

    return SamplingAudit(
        population_mean=population_mean,
        convenience_mean=convenience_mean,
        random_sample_mean=random_sample_mean,
        convenience_error=convenience_mean - population_mean,
        random_sample_error=random_sample_mean - population_mean,
        random_indices=tuple(int(index) for index in random_indices_array),
    )
```

For one seed, the random sample may be closer to 10 than the convenience sample
or farther from it. That is not a contradiction. The derivation concerns the
average over the sampling design, while this function produces one realised
sample. A later simulation can repeat the function across seeds and inspect the
distribution of estimates.

### Exact published tests

```python
import unittest

import numpy as np


class StatisticalEvidenceTests(unittest.TestCase):
    def test_mean_known_example(self) -> None:
        self.assertEqual(mean_from_scratch([4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 20]), 10.0)

    def test_mean_rejects_empty_input(self) -> None:
        with self.assertRaisesRegex(ValueError, "at least one"):
            mean_from_scratch([])

    def test_mean_rejects_boolean(self) -> None:
        with self.assertRaisesRegex(TypeError, "not bool"):
            mean_from_scratch([1.0, True])

    def test_mean_normalizes_huge_integer_failure(self) -> None:
        with self.assertRaisesRegex(TypeError, "representable"):
            mean_from_scratch([10**1000])

    def test_audit_exposes_convenience_error(self) -> None:
        result = sampling_audit([4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 20], 3, seed=7)
        self.assertEqual(result.population_mean, 10.0)
        self.assertEqual(result.convenience_mean, 6.0)
        self.assertEqual(result.convenience_error, -4.0)

    def test_audit_is_deterministic_and_without_replacement(self) -> None:
        first = sampling_audit(np.arange(12.0), 5, seed=19)
        second = sampling_audit(np.arange(12.0), 5, seed=19)
        self.assertEqual(first.random_indices, second.random_indices)
        self.assertEqual(len(set(first.random_indices)), 5)

    def test_audit_rejects_nonfinite_outcome(self) -> None:
        with self.assertRaisesRegex(ValueError, "finite"):
            sampling_audit([1.0, np.nan], 1)

    def test_audit_rejects_invalid_sample_size(self) -> None:
        with self.assertRaisesRegex(ValueError, "between 1"):
            sampling_audit([1.0, 2.0], 3)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    unittest.main()
```

Expected success summary:

```text
Ran 8 tests

OK
```

The tests establish the published functions' behaviour for selected normal and
failure cases. They do not establish that a real operational sample is random,
that the measurement is valid, or that the observed difference is causal.

### From notebook to evidence package

A production analysis should keep:

- an immutable input snapshot or reproducible query;
- a data dictionary and provenance record;
- inclusion and exclusion counts;
- the estimand card and analysis plan;
- dependency and runtime versions;
- validation results and failed checks;
- code, tests, configuration, and seed policy;
- tables generated from code rather than hand-edited;
- a claim-to-evidence ledger.

For sensitive operational data, this does not mean publishing raw records.
Reproducibility can use controlled access, aggregate outputs, synthetic
examples, checksums, query identifiers, and privacy review.

## 06 Learn from the ways an analysis fails

### Selection bias

Selection bias occurs when inclusion is related to the outcome or to variables
that affect the comparison, and the analysis does not account for that
mechanism. Mira's day-shift pilot has different engineers and incident patterns
from the old-summary group. The six-minute difference entangles summary,
shift, training, severity, and logging changes.

Random sampling addresses representation of a defined population. Random
assignment addresses comparability of treatment policies under a causal
design. The two ideas solve different problems. A randomized sample with
non-random treatment assignment can represent the population yet yield a
confounded treatment comparison. A randomized experiment on a narrow volunteer
sample can estimate an internal effect for that sample while generalization
remains uncertain.

### Measurement drift

When definitions or instruments change over time, a trend can reflect the
measurement system. Store schema versions and transformation versions with the
data. Run overlap periods when possible. Compare raw events under both rules.
Do not “standardize” incompatible definitions by renaming columns.

### Leakage

Leakage occurs when an analysis uses information unavailable at the intended
decision time or transfers information across evaluation boundaries. Examples
include:

- computing a feature from an incident's final resolution;
- fitting preprocessing on the full dataset before a train/test split;
- allowing duplicate incidents to appear in both development and evaluation;
- selecting a metric after inspecting which one improved;
- repeatedly checking a holdout set until it becomes part of development.

Leakage is a timing and information-flow problem. A variable can be
statistically associated with the outcome and still be invalid for the
intended use.

### Missing data and censoring

A missing value is not a single state. It may mean “not measured,” “not
applicable,” “sensor failed,” “user declined,” “event has not happened yet,”
or “record was removed.” Mira's unresolved alerts do not have a final triage
time because the outcome process is incomplete. Dropping them changes the
population to resolved alerts and may make the new summary look faster if it
leaves difficult alerts unresolved.

Chapter 10 develops missing-data mechanisms and sensitivity analysis. Chapter
1 requires a missingness dictionary and an accounting table:

```text
eligible incidents
  -> included incidents
  -> outcome observed
  -> outcome analysed
```

Every transition needs a count and reason.

### Simpson's paradox and aggregation

An overall comparison can move in a different direction from comparisons
within relevant groups. If the new summary is used mostly for easy incidents
and the old summary mostly for severe incidents, the overall mean can improve
even if the new summary is slower within each severity group.

This does not mean analysts should automatically condition on every column.
Grouping variables must be justified by design, timing, and the estimand.
Conditioning on a post-exposure variable can create a different bias.

### Exploratory-confirmatory blur

Exploratory analysis is essential for finding errors, learning distributions,
and generating hypotheses. Confirmatory analysis asks whether a prespecified
claim survives a defined procedure. Trouble begins when an exploratory choice
is reported as though it had been fixed in advance.

Suppose Mira tries twelve outcome definitions, five exclusions, and four
subgroups, then reports the one comparison with the largest improvement. The
final number does not carry the same error guarantees as one prespecified
analysis. The solution is not to forbid exploration. Label it, preserve the
analysis trail, and confirm important findings on new data or with an
appropriate multiplicity-aware design.

The [TOP Guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aab2374) describe
transparency practices for research materials, data, and design. The
[APA statistical-methods guidance](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.54.8.594)
emphasizes assumptions, effect sizes, uncertainty, and complete reporting.
These sources support a general principle: a statistical result is more
credible when readers can reconstruct how the question, data, analysis, and
claim were connected.

### Common language failures

Avoid these shortcuts:

- “The data say” when the data were produced by a selective process.
- “Significant” without saying statistical, practical, or operational meaning.
- “Random data” when only a pseudorandom split was used.
- “Representative” without naming the population and selection design.
- “The model caused” when the evidence is observational.
- “No effect” when the analysis merely failed to detect one.
- “Unbiased data” when only one estimator has an unbiasedness result under assumptions.

Precise language is part of method, not decoration.

## 07 Evaluate the evidence, cost, and responsibility

### A claim-to-evidence ladder

Different claims require different evidence.

| Claim | Minimum evidence direction |
|---|---|
| “The pilot records have a lower mean” | Stable measurement, transparent exclusions, reproducible descriptive calculation |
| “The pilot groups differ after adjustment” | Prespecified model, overlap and diagnostics, uncertainty, sensitivity to specification |
| “Assigning the new summary reduces triage time” | Defensible causal design or assumptions, assignment integrity, outcome completeness, effect and uncertainty |
| “The change will help next quarter” | Transportability argument, future-population monitoring, operational guardrails |
| “The system is safe and fair” | Multi-dimensional risk evidence, subgroup uncertainty, governance, monitoring, human escalation; never one metric |

Moving up the ladder requires new design evidence, not stronger adjectives.

### Evaluate the data pipeline before the estimator

For Mira's analysis, useful checks include:

1. eligible incident counts by period and group;
2. duplicate and linkage rates;
3. logger and schema versions;
4. missing outcome rates and reasons;
5. exposure assignment by shift, severity, and engineer;
6. timestamp ordering and impossible durations;
7. subgroup sample sizes;
8. analysis-plan timestamp relative to outcome inspection.

Only after those checks should the team interpret a difference of means.

### Computational complexity

`mean_from_scratch` visits each of $n$ values once, so its time complexity is
$\Theta(n)$ and additional space is $\Theta(1)$. The NumPy audit converts the
full population to a `float64` array, validates it, and samples indices. Its
dominant time and storage are $\Theta(N)$ for this teaching implementation.

At production scale, avoid loading an unbounded population into memory merely
to compute aggregates. Use validated streaming aggregates, database queries,
or distributed reductions with reconciliation checks. A fast distributed mean
still inherits the definition and selection problems of its inputs.

Floating-point reduction order can change the last digits. Record dtype and
library versions when small numerical differences matter. For financial,
scientific, or safety-critical quantities, select an accumulation policy that
matches the required error budget.

### Scalability is also organisational

A statistical workflow must survive changing schemas, rotating teams, and
repeated decisions. Version:

- metric definitions;
- eligibility rules;
- unit-of-analysis logic;
- data contracts;
- analysis plans;
- code and environments;
- report templates;
- approval decisions.

Monitoring should detect sample-ratio mismatch, missingness changes,
measurement drift, distribution shifts, and guardrail deterioration. Logging
more personal data “just in case” is not a neutral scalability strategy.

### Privacy, fairness, and security

An observational unit can represent a person even when the table uses an
opaque identifier. Collect only fields needed for the declared purpose. Apply
access controls, retention limits, aggregation, and privacy review. Never copy
raw sensitive values into logs, test fixtures, or published notebooks.

Fairness questions also depend on estimands and measurement. A subgroup mean
with five observations is not stable evidence. A missing demographic field may
reflect refusal, system design, or structural exclusion. Aggregated equality
can hide harmful error patterns. Report subgroup denominators, uncertainty,
data limitations, and the consequences of action.

Security affects validity. An attacker or failing client can generate duplicate
events, impossible timestamps, extreme values, or selectively missing
outcomes. Input validation, lineage, anomaly monitoring, and immutable audit
logs protect both systems and statistical conclusions.

### Answer guidance

1. Rows can be repeated or nested measurements from the same unit; the unit is
   defined by the question and design.
2. The population is the claim target, the frame is the operational source of
   selectable units, and the sample is what was observed.
3. The estimand is the population target, the estimator is the data-to-number
   rule, and the estimate is one realised number.
4. In the derivation, every fixed-size subset is equally likely under simple
   random sampling and the measurement is stable.
5. More selectively collected data can concentrate around the wrong target.
6. A logger that starts one group's clock later creates a systematic recorded
   improvement without changing real behaviour.
7. Sampling supports population representation; assignment supports policy
   comparability under a causal design.
8. Complete cases may define a selected population related to difficulty or
   outcome.
9. Exploratory choices respond to observed data; confirmatory procedures fix
   the main claim and method in advance or account for adaptation.
10. She needs a defensible assignment or causal identification strategy,
    common measurement, outcome accounting, integrity checks, uncertainty, and
    guardrails.
11. Correlated rows do not provide the same information as independent units.
12. The tests support function behaviour for selected inputs. They do not test
    real selection, measurement validity, independence, or causality.

### Complete chapter summary

Statistical reasoning begins by defining meaning. A target population names
where the claim should apply. An observational unit says what is counted once.
A sampling frame records who or what could enter the study. A sample records
what actually entered. Variables need conceptual definitions, units, timing,
source versions, and missing-value semantics.

An estimand is a population target. An estimator is a rule applied to sample
data. An estimate is one result. Keeping them distinct prevents a dashboard
number from quietly becoming a universal claim.

Under simple random sampling from a fixed finite population, the sample mean
is unbiased for the population mean because every unit has inclusion
probability $n/N$. This is a property of the sampling design over repetitions,
not a promise that one sample equals the population and not a theorem about
convenience data.

Selection bias, measurement drift, leakage, missingness, duplicated units, and
exploratory flexibility can all produce plausible but misleading results.
Tests and code validate computations under their contracts; they do not prove
that operational data satisfy the design assumptions.

Mira's six-minute difference is therefore the beginning of an investigation,
not its conclusion. The evidence map tells her how to design the next study,
what to preserve, and how to report the current pilot honestly.
