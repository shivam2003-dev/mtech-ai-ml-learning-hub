## 01. Begin with the security question, not the algorithm

### The one-minute version

Security teams rarely ask for “a classifier.” They ask questions such as:
“Which login events deserve review?”, “Which package releases deserve a second
look?”, or “Which traffic windows deserve investigation?” Each question has a
protected asset, an adversary or failure mode, an observation process, a person
who can act, and a cost when the action is wrong. Machine learning can rank or
classify observations, but it cannot supply those missing definitions.

Imagine Noor, an analyst responsible for a small organisation's software
inventory. A weekly scanner produces records containing a package name, a
version, an advisory severity, whether the package is internet-facing, and the
number of systems on which it appears. The manager asks Noor to “use AI to
find critical vulnerabilities.” Noor first asks what *critical* means. Does it
mean a high CVSS score, likely exploitation this week, a service that cannot be
patched quickly, or a combination? The answer changes the label, features,
evaluation period, and response.

The same discipline applies to malware, phishing, traffic, and user behaviour.
A model may estimate a score; a security process turns that score into an alert,
queue position, containment action, or no action. The chapter's central claim
is modest but powerful: write the evidence contract before selecting the model.

### Mira's running example

Mira receives one record per login attempt. The approved defensive goal is to
prioritise unusual attempts for human review. The system is not authorised to
lock an account automatically. Features are a synthetic, privacy-safe vector:
hour-of-day, a binary indicator for a new device, a binary indicator for a new
country, and a count of recent failed attempts. A historical investigation label
$y=1$ means “an analyst confirmed this attempt as requiring investigation under
the organisation's documented policy.” It does *not* mean “the user is a
criminal.”

The model emits a score $s=f_\theta(\mathbf{x})$. A threshold $\tau$ creates a
review queue, $\hat y=\mathbf{1}[s\ge\tau]$. The score is not a probability
unless calibration has been checked. A threshold is not an incident response
plan. These distinctions prevent a common and harmful slide from a statistical
signal to an accusation.

### What this chapter promises

You will build a terminology map, write the evidence contract, derive a
cost-sensitive threshold argument, implement a tiny baseline twice in spirit
(transparent rule and logistic model), inspect failure modes, and design a
bounded study lab. You will also see why the official later topics—gradient
boosting, graphs, malware, phishing, traffic, autoencoders, adversarial ML,
LLMs, and reinforcement learning—are model choices inside a common security
workflow rather than isolated magic tricks.

## 02. Map assets, threats, evidence, and actions

### A security event is not a label

An **asset** is something whose confidentiality, integrity, availability, or
accountability matters: an account, package, host, model, log stream, or
service. A **threat** is a potential cause of harm, including a malicious actor
or an accidental failure. A **vulnerability** is a weakness that can be
exploited or otherwise lead to harm. An **attack vector** is a route through
which that weakness is reached. An **observation** is what a sensor records;
it is always a partial view of reality. An **indicator** is a signal associated
with a hypothesis, not proof of intent. An **alert** is an operational decision
to ask someone to inspect an observation.

The distinctions matter mathematically. If the label is “analyst review was
required,” a model is learning the historical queue policy. If the label is
“confirmed compromise,” the dataset may contain only rare and delayed cases.
If a feature is copied from the remediation ticket created after discovery,
the model has access to future information. A large score can then be a
measurement of leakage rather than security.

### Threat-informed framing

MITRE ATT&CK provides a knowledge base for describing adversary tactics and
techniques. Its role in a learning project is to make the threat hypothesis
explicit and to provide a vocabulary for coverage; it is not a ready-made
training label. MITRE ATLAS applies a similar threat-informed perspective to
attacks on AI systems. NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 gives an organisational
structure for govern, identify, protect, detect, respond, and recover work.
NIST AI RMF 1.0 adds a lifecycle view of trustworthy AI. These frameworks do
not choose a model for Noor. They help her say what the model is meant to
support and what evidence is still missing.

### The four-way boundary

For each proposed feature, ask four questions. Is it available before the
decision? Is its collection authorised and privacy-respecting? Can an analyst
interpret or challenge it? Does it describe the event, or does it encode a
previous decision? A hostname can be useful and sensitive; a threat-intel
indicator can be stale; a user identifier can create discriminatory shortcuts.
Security data is not automatically safe to reuse because it came from a log.

## 03. Write the ML evidence contract

### A compact contract

An evidence contract is a short, testable statement containing:

1. **Decision:** what ranking, alert, or estimate is produced?
2. **Asset and threat:** what is protected and from which failure or adversary?
3. **Population:** whose events and which environments are included?
4. **Observation time:** when can each feature be known?
5. **Label:** who assigned it, under which policy, and with what delay?
6. **Action:** who reviews the result and what are they permitted to do?
7. **Metrics and costs:** which errors matter and how are they measured?
8. **Boundaries:** when does the model abstain, expire, or require escalation?

For Mira: “For login attempts from the approved identity provider during the
next calendar month, rank events for a human review queue using features known
at authentication time. Evaluate temporal generalisation and analyst workload.
The model may recommend review; it may not lock accounts or contact users.
Report recall at a fixed daily review budget, precision, calibration, subgroup
error analysis, and drift. Retrain or disable it when the data contract breaks.”
This is longer than “detect suspicious logins,” but every clause can be tested.

### From contract to table

Suppose a one-day evaluation has 10,000 attempts and 100 confirmed review
labels. A model raises 80 true positives, 40 false positives, 20 false
negatives, and 9,860 true negatives. Recall is $80/(80+20)=0.80$. Precision is
$80/(80+40)=0.667$. The accuracy, $(80+9860)/10000=0.994$, sounds impressive
and says little about the 100 cases that matter. A review budget of 120 alerts
also matters: adding more alerts can improve recall while exhausting analysts.

### Label delay and selective feedback

Security labels often arrive after investigation. Events a team did not review
cannot be treated as clean negatives; they are “unknown under this process.”
The queue itself changes future labels because reviewed events receive more
attention. A model trained on its own historical alerts can learn to reproduce
its blind spots. Keep an unlabeled or delayed slice, record the label source,
and avoid pretending that absence of an incident ticket proves benignity.

## 04. Derive decisions, thresholds, and a baseline

### Expected cost

Let $C_{FP}$ be the cost of reviewing a benign event, and $C_{FN}$ the cost of
missing a harmful event. For a threshold $\tau$, an empirical decision cost is

$$
\widehat C(\tau)=C_{FP}\,FP(\tau)+C_{FN}\,FN(\tau)+C_R\,R(\tau),
$$

where $C_R$ represents the marginal cost of review and $R(\tau)$ is the number
of alerts. This is not a universal economic truth; it is a transparent model
of a particular operational conversation. If a model returns a calibrated
probability $p=P(y=1\mid\mathbf{x})$ and the only choices are review or no
review, a simplified Bayes rule reviews when the expected cost of missing is
larger than the cost of review. In practice, calibration, changing prevalence,
and human workload make threshold selection an empirical exercise.

### A logistic baseline

For feature vector $\mathbf{x}$, weights $\mathbf{w}$, and intercept $b$,

$$
z=\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}+b,\qquad
p=\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}.
$$

With binary labels, the average log loss is

$$
\mathcal L(\mathbf{w},b)= -\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\left[y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\right].
$$

The gradient with respect to weights is
$\nabla_{\mathbf w}\mathcal L=\frac1n\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf p-\mathbf y)$
when rows of $\mathbf X$ are examples. This derivation is useful because it
connects an error on a review label to a parameter update; it does not confer
causal or forensic meaning on a coefficient. Standardisation, regularisation,
and a chronological split are part of the experiment, not afterthoughts.

### Transparent code

The following fixture is synthetic. It intentionally omits hostnames, payloads,
credentials, and network access. It uses a fixed seed and validates shapes.
The function returns probabilities, not accusations, and the chosen threshold
is supplied by the caller.

```python
from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Final

import numpy as np

Array: Final = np.ndarray


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class LogisticModel:
    weights: Array
    bias: float

    def predict_probability(self, features: Array) -> Array:
        x = np.asarray(features, dtype=np.float64)
        if x.ndim != 2 or x.shape[1] != self.weights.shape[0]:
            raise ValueError("features must have shape (n_examples, n_features)")
        logits = x @ self.weights + self.bias
        logits = np.clip(logits, -40.0, 40.0)
        return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-logits))


def fit_logistic(features: Array, labels: Array, steps: int = 1200,
                 learning_rate: float = 0.25) -> LogisticModel:
    x = np.asarray(features, dtype=np.float64)
    y = np.asarray(labels, dtype=np.float64)
    if x.ndim != 2 or y.ndim != 1 or x.shape[0] != y.size:
        raise ValueError("features must be 2-D and labels must match rows")
    if not np.all(np.isin(y, (0.0, 1.0))):
        raise ValueError("labels must be 0 or 1")
    if steps <= 0 or learning_rate <= 0:
        raise ValueError("steps and learning_rate must be positive")
    weights = np.zeros(x.shape[1], dtype=np.float64)
    bias = 0.0
    for _ in range(steps):
        probability = LogisticModel(weights, bias).predict_probability(x)
        error = probability - y
        weights -= learning_rate * (x.T @ error) / x.shape[0]
        bias -= learning_rate * float(error.mean())
    return LogisticModel(weights, bias)


def confusion_counts(labels: Array, probabilities: Array,
                    threshold: float = 0.5) -> tuple[int, int, int, int]:
    y = np.asarray(labels, dtype=np.int64)
    p = np.asarray(probabilities, dtype=np.float64)
    if y.ndim != 1 or p.shape != y.shape or not 0.0 <= threshold <= 1.0:
        raise ValueError("labels/probabilities shape or threshold is invalid")
    predicted = p >= threshold
    return (int(np.sum(predicted & (y == 1))),
            int(np.sum(predicted & (y == 0))),
            int(np.sum(~predicted & (y == 1))),
            int(np.sum(~predicted & (y == 0))))


rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
features = rng.normal(size=(240, 4))
logits = 1.3 * features[:, 1] + 0.9 * features[:, 2] - 0.4
labels = (logits > 0.0).astype(np.int64)
model = fit_logistic(features[:180], labels[:180])
probabilities = model.predict_probability(features[180:])
assert probabilities.shape == (60,)
tp, fp, fn, tn = confusion_counts(labels[180:], probabilities, threshold=0.6)
assert tp + fp + fn + tn == 60
print({"tp": tp, "fp": fp, "fn": fn, "tn": tn})
```

The leading training cost is $O(Tnd)$ for $T$ gradient steps, $n$ examples,
and $d$ features; prediction is $O(nd)$. Memory is $O(nd+d)$. These are
algorithmic costs, not promises about a production pipeline. The assertion
checks a shape and a conservation identity. It cannot check whether the label
policy is fair, the split is temporal, or an attacker will adapt.

## 05. Interpret evidence without overclaiming

### The split is part of the threat model

A random split can place near-duplicate files, sessions from one incident, or
future indicators in training and test. For vulnerability management, train on
older disclosures and test on newer ones. For traffic, hold out a later time
window or a network segment. For user behaviour, separate identities or
organisations when the deployment target is a new population. Report the
split rule in plain language and keep preprocessing fit only on training data.

### Metrics answer different questions

Precision asks how much review capacity is consumed by useful alerts. Recall
asks how much of the chosen label was surfaced. A precision-recall curve is
often more informative than ROC AUC for rare positives, but neither metric
specifies analyst time. Calibration asks whether a score of 0.8 means roughly
eight out of ten comparable cases are positive; it is not the same as
discrimination. A confusion matrix should be accompanied by prevalence,
threshold, time window, confidence interval or repeated-seed range, and a
description of unknown or delayed labels.

### Baselines are security controls

Compare the learned model with a documented rule, a recent-known-indicator
lookup, a random or prevalence baseline where appropriate, and a simple
linear model. A complex detector that does not beat the existing control under
the same review budget may increase operational risk. Conversely, a baseline
that performs well on an old benchmark may fail when an adversary changes a
file, domain, protocol, or user population. The question is not “is the model
accurate?” but “does this evidence justify this bounded action under this
threat model?”

### Security and privacy boundaries

Logs can contain credentials, personal data, precise location, and sensitive
business events. Minimise collection, hash identifiers only when the analysis
still works, restrict access, and document retention. A model that infers
“risky person” from nationality, shift, or job title can create discriminatory
surveillance while appearing statistically useful. Keep behaviour and identity
separate where possible, offer an appeal path, and never expose raw sensitive
records in a notebook or paper screenshot. Defensive research still requires
authorisation, responsible disclosure, and safe handling.

### What an analyst needs to see

An alert is useful only when it arrives with enough context to be challenged.
Mira's queue item should show the observation time, the features used, the
model version, the threshold, a plain-language reason for ranking, and a link
to the approved evidence—not an unexplained red badge. The explanation must be
honest about scope. A coefficient or feature attribution can describe what the
model used; it cannot prove that the feature caused the event. A system should
also allow an analyst to mark “insufficient evidence,” “benign change,” or
“needs more data.” Those outcomes are valuable labels for improving the process
and safer than forcing every observation into a binary accusation.

Operational context changes the mathematics. If one analyst can review 30
events an hour, a detector that emits 500 alerts is not simply “high recall.”
It is a queue with a predictable abandonment risk. If the most expensive error
is a missed compromise, a second independent signal or a staged review may be
better than lowering a threshold indefinitely. If a deployment serves several
teams, each team may have a different prevalence and action cost; a global
threshold can be unfairly calibrated. Treat threshold selection as a governed
policy with an owner, a review date, and an emergency disable path.

### Reproducibility as a control

Security experiments should preserve the data checksum, code revision, package
versions, random seed, split manifest, metric definitions, and decision
threshold. A colleague must be able to tell whether a claimed improvement came
from a new model or from a changed label window. Store rejected experiments and
negative results as well as successful runs. When a model is updated, compare
the old and new queue on a fixed holdout and inspect changed decisions. This is
not bureaucracy: an untraceable detector is difficult to investigate after a
miss, and an unrepeatable benchmark can hide a regression.

## 06. Connect the introduction to the official techniques

The later course topics are variations on the same contract. **Gradient
boosting for vulnerability management** is appropriate when tabular advisory,
asset, exploitability, and exposure features are available; it still needs a
time-aware label and remediation-cost definition. **Graph-based static
analysis** and **graph neural networks** represent code, call graphs, or
attack paths; graph construction and family leakage can dominate model choice.
**Supervised and deep malware detection** requires a safe feature pipeline,
concept-drift plan, and a distinction between file family and maliciousness.

**NLP and Transformers for phishing** must avoid treating a sender's language,
accent, or legitimate urgency as evidence of intent. **Deep traffic analysis**
must state encryption, aggregation window, sampling bias, and whether the
deployment sees the same network. **Autoencoders for user/entity behaviour**
learn a notion of normality that may encode an already-compromised baseline;
anomaly is not guilt. **Adversarial ML** asks how data, queries, or model
interfaces can be manipulated and which mitigations have evidence. **LLMs in
security operations** can summarise or retrieve with approval but may invent
facts, leak secrets, or follow hostile instructions. **Reinforcement learning
for response** is a control problem with safety constraints; a lab policy must
not quarantine real hosts or delete data.

## 07. Study the failure modes and research connection

### Six predictable failures

First, a label is defined after the event. The model then predicts a ticket
workflow rather than risk. Second, a feature leaks the future through a
remediation field, threat-intelligence update, or post-incident tag. Third, a
random split lets a campaign family appear in both sides. Fourth, alert volume
is ignored, so a detector wins recall by overwhelming analysts. Fifth, an
unbalanced benchmark hides a poor minority-class result. Sixth, automation
silently converts a score into a punitive action. Each failure is a contract
failure before it is an algorithm failure.

### Recommended reading path

Start with the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 and NIST AI RMF 1.0 for
governance vocabulary. Read MITRE ATT&CK to understand threat-informed
coverage and MITRE ATLAS for attacks against ML systems. Read Anderson and
Roth's EMBER paper for a concrete malware benchmark, but inspect its collection
period and feature assumptions. Read Du et al.'s DeepLog paper for sequence
anomaly detection and ask which log process was treated as normal. Use Choi et
al. and Macas et al. as survey maps, not as substitutes for primary studies.

The papers' claims remain their claims. This chapter's interpretation is that
security ML is a measurement-and-decision problem under an adaptive threat;
that is an educational synthesis, not a result reported by each source.

## 08. Run the bounded study lab and assignment

### Practical exercise

Generate the fixture in the code block and record the random seed, split, four
confusion counts, and threshold. Then change only the threshold from 0.6 to
0.3. Predict how review volume, precision, and recall will move before running
the code. Add a chronological synthetic drift by shifting one feature in the
last 60 rows. Write whether the model should be recalibrated, retrained,
disabled, or left unchanged and why. Do not add real user identifiers.

### Assignment

Write a two-page evidence contract for one defensive use case: vulnerability
prioritisation, phishing triage, malware feature classification, or log anomaly
ranking. Include asset, threat, observation window, label authority, allowed
action, privacy boundary, temporal split, baseline, metric at a fixed review
budget, and a rollback trigger. Implement one deterministic baseline and one
linear model on synthetic or approved public data. Test at least one leakage
scenario and one prevalence shift. The deliverable must include a data card,
model card, confusion matrix, error examples with sensitive fields removed, and
a marking rubric: contract 25%, data and split 20%, implementation/tests 20%,
cost-aware evidence 20%, safety and communication 15%.

### Quiz and solution guidance

1. Why can 99% accuracy be weak in a rare-event detector? **Because prevalence
   and the cost of missed positives are hidden; compute precision, recall, and
   workload on the relevant population.**
2. Is an autoencoder anomaly score a proof of compromise? **No. It measures
   deviation from its learned reference distribution; investigate drift,
   novelty, and benign operational changes.**
3. What makes a random split unsafe for a campaign dataset? **Near-duplicate or
   related events can cross the split, giving future or family information to
   training.**
4. Why must a probability threshold be tuned with an action budget? **Because
   the same score distribution can imply very different analyst capacity and
   false-positive cost.**
5. What is the safest default for an unreviewed ML alert? **A reversible,
   human-reviewed queue entry with an explicit abstention path—not autonomous
   containment.**

## 09. Defend the chapter in an oral exam

### Questions an examiner may ask

Explain the difference between an asset, vulnerability, threat, indicator, and
alert using one login example. Derive the logistic gradient and state its
shape assumptions. Why is a score not automatically a probability? Design a
temporal split for vulnerability data and identify two leakage paths. Compare
precision at a fixed queue budget with recall at a fixed threshold. Explain how
MITRE ATT&CK helps a threat hypothesis without becoming a label generator.
Name one privacy risk in user/entity analytics and one safe mitigation. Finally,
state when you would disable the model even if its offline metric improved.

### Complete summary

AI and ML in cybersecurity begins with a defensible question. The protected
asset, threat, observation process, label, decision owner, allowed action,
error costs, and boundaries define what a model is allowed to mean. A logistic
baseline is valuable because it makes shapes, gradients, thresholds, and tests
visible. Temporal splits, delayed labels, workload, privacy, drift, and
adversarial adaptation are part of evidence, not operational decoration.

The next chapter introduces vulnerabilities, threats, attack vectors, and
defensive data in greater detail. Keep this contract beside it: every later
architecture is only as trustworthy as the question and evidence it serves.
